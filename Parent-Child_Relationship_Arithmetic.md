# Parent–Child Relationship Arithmetic

*How Parents Fail to Update Their Model of a Child Who Has Become an Adult*

# 

## Preface

This is not a book about good parents or bad parents, and it offers no advice on raising children. It is about a single, ordinary failure of the mind — the difficulty of updating our picture of a person while that person keeps changing — examined through the one relationship in which the failure is most universal and hardest to see from the inside: a parent and a grown child.

The organizing image is arithmetic. Between a parent and a child, one quantity never changes: the difference in their ages. A parent who is twenty-five years older at the child's birth is twenty-five years older at the child's fortieth birthday. But almost everything that difference *means* does change — and this book's question is whether what meaning tracks is not the difference at all but the ratio, which collapses from a parent many times older than an infant to a parent barely older than a middle-aged adult, while the difference never moves. The book is named for that gap between what stays fixed and what quietly shifts. "Arithmetic" is the lens through which it looks, not a theorem it claims to have proved.

A word on how the central idea is offered. It is a candidate, not a law. Where the book leans on established work — in psychology, in the study of perception — it says so plainly, rather than dressing borrowed ideas as discoveries. And it tries to do the thing most books built around an idea avoid: to state, out loud, what would show the idea to be wrong. A model that cannot say how it might fail is not a model. It is a mood.

The scope is narrow on purpose. This book explains, at most, one mechanism in one kind of relationship. It does not explain the world, and its narrowness is not a weakness to apologize for — it is the only reason the idea can be tested at all.

The aim, then, is not to judge parents. It is to notice something small and nearly invisible, and to ask an uncomfortable question: not whether our children have grown up, but whether our picture of them has.

## Chapter 1 · The Frozen Image

A woman in her forties sits at her mother's table. She runs a department of thirty people; that morning she approved a budget larger than the house she grew up in. The waiter — it is a restaurant, her mother's choice — comes to take the order, and before she can speak, her mother answers for her. "She'll have the fish. She doesn't do well with heavy food." It is said warmly, without a flicker of doubt, the way you would speak for a child too young to read the menu. The daughter says nothing. She is forty-three years old, and she has just felt herself become nine.

Nothing in that moment is cruel. The mother loves her daughter and would be wounded to hear the scene described as anything but care. And it is care — which is exactly what makes it hard to name. It happens in every family, in a thousand small forms, and almost everyone recognizes it without being taught. The parent who still ends the conversation with "let me handle it." Who introduces a grown son to a stranger as "the shy one." Who reminds a fifty-year-old to drive carefully, to eat something, to dress warmly, as though the intervening decades were a rumor. Who says, meaning it as tenderness, *you will always be my baby.*

At the largest scale the same thing surfaces in statistics — adults who remain, well into their thirties, folded into the household and the authority of their parents; in some countries, more than half of them.[^v7] That number gets explained in many ways, most of them economic, and this book is not about the number. It is only the most visible symptom of something that also happens in families where the child left home at eighteen and lives a thousand miles away. The distance changes. The stance does not.

It would be easy to assume the trouble is simply that parents misjudge what their grown children can do. Sometimes they do — a parent underestimates a capability, and correcting the estimate would end the friction. But there is a stranger version, and it is the one worth a whole book, because it does not yield to any correction of the facts.

Listen to how adult children describe it, and the same shape appears again and again: *She knows exactly who I am. She knows I run a company, that I raised my own children, that people twice my age ask my advice. And she still tells me what to do. She still speaks to me as though I were fifteen.* The parent's factual picture is not wrong. Asked directly, she would list her daughter's accomplishments accurately, and with pride. What has not moved is something else — the daughter's *place.* She is still positioned as the junior, the one who is spoken for, whose competence is real but somehow does not count.

That is the thing to hold onto, because it is genuinely odd. Two things that ought to travel together have come apart. What the parent *knows* about the child has been updated across forty years. Where the parent *puts* the child has not moved at all — and it is not moved by any new fact, because it was never made of facts in the first place.

So the question of this book is narrower and stranger than "why are some parents controlling." That question has answers, and they are mostly about the parent — anxiety, habit, the long reflex of protection. This is a different question, and it is not about the parent's motives at all. Something is being held constant that has nothing to do with what the child can do, and the rest of this book is an attempt to find out what that something is.

There is a clue, and it is almost too plain to notice. In this one relationship, among everything that has changed, exactly one number has stayed the same. Perhaps that is where to look.

## Chapter 2 · What the Field Already Knows

Before going any further, I owe the reader an admission and a debt. The admission is that the thing described in the last chapter — a parent whose picture of a grown child stays fixed while the facts move on — is not a discovery of mine. It has been studied, carefully, for sixty years. The debt is to say so plainly, and to lay out what that body of work found, in its strongest form, before I claim that there is anything left to add. A book that introduces its own idea without first showing the reader the field it stands on is not bold. It is merely hoping you have not read the field.

The work begins with Margaret Blenkner,[^v5] who in 1965 gave a name to a change she saw in the relationship between aging parents and their adult children. She called it *filial maturity*: the point at which a grown child stops relating to a parent as a child relates to a caretaker, and begins to meet them as one adult meets another — someone with their own history, their own limits, their own needs. It was offered as a developmental stage, a transition that becomes possible when the child is grown, and it named something real that most people feel but few have words for.

For a long time the research followed the child's side of that transition. It described the adult child learning to *de-idealize* the parent — to stop seeing them as the towering figure of early childhood and start seeing a person, fallible and particular. It described *individuation*, the slow work of becoming a self distinct from the family that made you. This is a rich and well-mapped literature, and it is worth saying that it is largely about the direction opposite to the one this book takes. It is about how the child updates their picture of the parent.

But the field did not stop there, and this is the part that matters most for what follows, because it is the part that comes closest to the subject of this book. Researchers noticed that filial maturity has a counterpart on the other side of the relationship. If the child is learning to see the parent as a person, then there is a parallel task on the parent's side: learning to see the grown child as an autonomous adult rather than as the child they raised. The idea, as the literature summarizes it, is that parents ideally come to accept that a son or daughter is no longer a child but an autonomous individual — a formulation the field attributes to William Aquilino's[^v6] 2006 work on family relationships in emerging adulthood.[^v1] The field went further and built instruments to measure this parental side — a *parental maturity* scale, an attempt to put a number on how fully a given parent has made that adjustment. It has even been described, in the words of one review of the literature, as the *other half of the picture* — the *matched pair* of filial maturity.[^v2] The phenomenon of the last chapter, in other words, is not an unclaimed continent. It has a name and a measure of its own, and it descends from a research tradition six decades deep — though the parent's side of that tradition, the side this book is about, is considerably younger and thinner than the child's, dating from the mid-2000s rather than 1965.

I want to be as fair to this work as I can, because the temptation in a book like this is to describe the existing field weakly so that one's own contribution looks larger by contrast. So let me state the field's account at its strongest. It says: there is a developmental achievement, available to parents of grown children, in which the parent comes to relate to the child as a fellow adult. Parents vary in how fully they reach it. Those who reach it more fully tend to have warmer, more reciprocal relationships with their children; those who reach it less fully tend to have more friction. The achievement is correlated with the health of the relationship, and it can be measured. That is a real finding, honestly earned, and much of what the last chapter described is simply an instance of it. The mother at the restaurant, ordering for her forty-three-year-old daughter, is a parent who — in the field's terms — has not fully reached parental maturity. The field saw her first.

So what is left? If the phenomenon is claimed, what is this book for?

Here I have to be careful, and slower than the momentum of the argument would like, because the honest answer is narrow, and it is narrower still once the field is described accurately. It is not that the field missed the phenomenon. It did not. And it is not — I have to correct a temptation of my own here — that the field sees only one flat dimension. It does not. The literature on this transition already knows that adult status is not a single switch: a young adult can arrive at adulthood in some domains and not in others, and one influential account frames the whole passage as *recentering* — a three-stage developmental process in which a dynamic shift of power and agency moves the young person out of dependence on the family and toward self-determined independence.[^v3] So I cannot honestly say the field treats this as one flat thing. It does not.

What I can say is narrower than I first thought, and the narrowing came from checking the instrument. The child's side of this transition was given a developmental model of its own — recentering, the staged shift of power and agency away from dependence. The parent's side was given a measure too, and I had assumed it collapsed the parent's adjustment into a single score; it does not.[^v4] The parental maturity measure resolves into *two* factors — one for grasping the grown child as an independent adult with their own limits and needs, and one for releasing control. So I cannot claim the parent's side is modelled at lower resolution than the child's; it is modelled on two dimensions of its own. What I can still ask is a narrower question, and it is the one this book turns on: those two factors are both about the parent's *adjustment* — comprehending, and letting go. Neither of them is the split this book is after, between what a parent factually *knows* about a child and where a parent *places* that child. The question is not whether the parent's side has dimensions. It has two. It is whether anyone has modelled *these particular two* — knowledge and placement, updated by different processes and able to come apart — rather than the two we already have. That question I have not yet closed, and I will not pretend the instrument settles it.

But look again at the scene that started this book, because it does not quite fit a single score. The mother who orders for her daughter is not a parent who has failed to notice that her daughter grew up. Asked directly, she would describe her daughter's life accurately and with pride — the department of thirty, the budget, the grandchildren she raised. The knowledge is fully current. It is *only the stance* that has not moved. And that is a strange thing to compress into one maturity score, because it means one part of her has made the adjustment and another part has not — and the two parts are not the same part. What she knows has updated. Where she places her daughter has not. If a parent's adjustment were a single quantity, you would not expect to find it split down the middle like this, current on one side and frozen on the other, in the same parent, at the same table.

That is the crack this book tries to look into. Not the phenomenon — the field has that. But the possibility that what a maturity score compresses into one number is actually two things that usually move together and can come apart: what a parent *knows* about a child, and where a parent *places* a child. Two representations, updated by two different processes, at two different speeds. Most of the time they travel together, and when they do, a single score describes them well enough. The interesting cases — the restaurant, the fifty-year-old reminded to dress warmly — are the ones where they have separated, and where a single number cannot describe what you are seeing, because there is no single number: there are two, and they disagree.

I want to be honest about the status of that claim, because it is not yet earned, and the discipline of this book is to say so rather than to let momentum carry it. That the phenomenon is real, I am sure of — it is in the field, and it is at the table. That the right way to see it is as a *dissociation between two representations* rather than as a single adjustment scored on one measure — that is a reframing, not a fact, and whether the existing literature has already drawn this distinction somewhere I have not looked is a question I have not fully closed. It is a debt I am carrying forward openly, to be paid before this book makes its strongest claim, not after. What I can say now is only this: the field found the phenomenon, named it, and built a measure that reports it as one number. The rest of this book asks whether it is one thing.

And there is that clue again, the one from the end of the last chapter, which neither of the two maturity factors has any particular use for but which will not leave me alone. Among everything that changed between this parent and this child across forty years, one quantity did not. The next chapter is about that number, and about whether it has anything to do with which of the two representations moves and which one stays.

## Chapter 3 · The Arithmetic

Take the number first, before anything is claimed for it.

### 3.1 · The Geometric Observation

Between a parent and a child, the difference in their ages is fixed at birth and never moves again. Call it Δ. A mother twenty-five years older than her daughter on the day of the birth is twenty-five years older at the daughter's fortieth birthday, and at her own funeral. Of everything in that relationship, Δ is the only true constant.

But watch what happens to the *ratio* of their ages. When the child is one, a parent twenty-five years older is twenty-six — twenty-six times the child's age. At five, six times. At twenty-five, exactly twice. At fifty, one and a half times. Write it as a formula and the ratio is r(a) = 1 + Δ/a, where a is the child's age: a quantity that starts enormous and collapses toward one, forever approaching a world in which parent and child are, proportionally, almost the same age — two adults separated by a rounding error.

And the collapse itself is not steady. It is violent early and glacial late. Between the child's first and fifth birthdays, the ratio falls from twenty-six to six; between forty-five and fifty, it drifts from about 1.56 to 1.5. Formally, the slope of the ratio has magnitude Δ/a², which shrinks as the child ages: the same five years that once redrew the entire proportion between two people eventually barely move it. One consequence follows directly, and it matters for everything after: if a parent's picture of the child lags behind by some fixed amount of time, the *ratio error* that lag produces is largest when the child is young — and it is proportional to Δ. A model frozen for five years distorts a nine-year-old beyond recognition and a forty-nine-year-old almost not at all; and, for the same child, a larger age gap produces a proportionally larger distortion. Hold on to that last clause. It is the only thing in this chapter that will later be asked to earn its keep through data.

Everything in the last three paragraphs is arithmetic. It is true by derivation, the way it is true that a triangle's angles sum to two right angles. No experiment can refute it, no survey can weaken it, and — this must be said with equal force — no experiment can *confirm* it either, because it makes no claim yet about any mind. It would fall only if the calculus were wrong.

And none of it is mine. The mathematics of ratio-to-accumulated-time is at least a hundred and fifty years old. Paul Janet proposed in 1877 that a stretch of time is felt in proportion to the life already lived — a year at twenty is a twentieth of one's life, a year at eighty an eightieth — and William James gave the idea its lasting circulation.[^v8] Beneath both sits an older and broader finding, the Weber–Fechner regularity: across the senses, perception tends to track ratios rather than absolute differences. A candle added to a dark room is an event; the same candle added to a bright one is nothing. What this book does is not discover a law. It transposes one — from the perception of time and light and weight to a domain where, to my knowledge, it has not been formally placed: the perceived seniority between a parent and a child. The law is old. The transposition is the only thing on trial.

One more restraint before moving on. Even if perception of relational standing did track this ratio, the geometry by itself would predict no error at all. It predicts *susceptibility* to error: how much distortion a given amount of model-lag would produce, if there is lag. Whether there is lag — whether the parent's model actually freezes — is a separate, psychological fact, the one Chapter 1 described and the field has measured. Geometry sets the sensitivity. Psychology decides whether the sensitivity is ever cashed.

### 3.2 · The Two Channels

Now return to the mother at the restaurant, because the scene contains a structural fact that the arithmetic did not put there and does not yet explain.

There are at least two things about her daughter that the mother might be said to represent. One is what the daughter *can do* — manage money, run a department, choose her own dinner. Call it the capability channel. The other is where the daughter *stands* — who decides, who is spoken for, who may still be instructed to dress warmly. Call it the position channel. The scene from Chapter 1 is interesting precisely because the two have come apart in it: the capability file is accurate and current, and the position has not moved. Whatever else is true, the scene is evidence that the two *can* be held apart — that position is separable from capability, at least sometimes, in at least one direction.

The two channels also appear to be built from different material. Capability is episodic. It is assembled from incidents — the loan repaid, the company grown, the crisis handled — and it is revised by incidents, which is why it stays current: life keeps supplying evidence, and the file keeps absorbing it. When a capability judgment *does* freeze — "at fifteen he couldn't manage money, so not now" — the freezing has a well-understood mechanism: the internal working models of attachment theory, templates laid down early and resistant to revision. Position is different. "You will always be my child" cites no incident. It is not a summary of evidence, and no new evidence addresses it, which is exactly why Chapter 1 found that it "does not yield to any correction of the facts." Position looks less like a belief and more like an assignment.

I am deliberately claiming nothing here about what *governs* the position channel — not yet, and not in this section. This section makes only an existence claim: capability and position are distinct representations that can update independently, because we have watched them do so. What sets the position — whether it is the ratio of the last section, the episodic working models just described, or something with no arithmetic in it at all — is the question the rest of this chapter is built to keep honest. And one debt from Chapter 2 stands squarely across the path and is not lifted here: the field's two parental factors, comprehending and letting go, are both dimensions of the parent's *adjustment*, and whether anyone has already modelled this particular pair — knowledge and placement, as two representations moving at two speeds — is a question I have not closed.[^v4] Nothing in this chapter presumes the answer.

### 3.3 · The Competing Explanation

Before the arithmetic is allowed to explain anything, it must survive a rival that explains almost everything — and the rival deserves to be built at full strength, because if it is built weakly, any victory over it is fake.

Here is the rival. A child's actual capabilities follow a curve of their own: explosive early, saturating late. The difference between what a five-year-old and a ten-year-old can do is a chasm; the difference between thirty-five and forty is barely visible. Call the curve C(a), and notice that its slope, too, shrinks with age. Now give the parent nothing exotic — no ratios, no geometry — just an ordinary, slightly lagging track of the child's real abilities. What follows? A model frozen for five years misrepresents a young child catastrophically, because five years once *meant* a catastrophe of change, and misrepresents a middle-aged child hardly at all, because the child hardly changed. "The same freeze hurts more when young" falls out immediately — with no ratio anywhere in the machinery.

Add the second rival, already met in Chapter 2: the parent's side of this transition is not unmeasured. A validated instrument already resolves it into two dimensions — comprehending the child as an independent adult, and letting go of control. A reasonable reviewer will therefore put the question in its sharpest form: *the age-gradient is explained by maturation, the persistence is explained by working models, and the parent's adjustment is already modelled on two dimensions. What work is left for your arithmetic to do?* If I cannot make that question feel unanswerable for a page, I have not stated the rival honestly. So let it stand: as of this paragraph, a reader is entitled to suspect that the ratio is decoration — a vivid restatement of things the field already owns.

### 3.4 · Why the Two Are Easily Confused

The suspicion has a precise source, and it should be confessed rather than managed.

In every ordinary family, the two curves move together. When the child is young, the ratio's slope is steep *and* the maturation curve is steep; when the child is grown, both have flattened. The one prediction that has been doing intuitive work since Chapter 1 — that a frozen image costs most when the child is young — is therefore *over-determined*: both mechanisms make it, in the same direction, at the same ages. In the normal case the prediction cannot tell them apart, which means that everything in this book so far that felt like evidence for the arithmetic is equally consistent with the rival. A prediction both sides make identifies neither. If this chapter stopped here, the honest verdict would be: the arithmetic is a metaphor with good manners, and the mechanisms are maturation and memory.

It does not stop here, because the two mechanisms differ in exactly one place, and the difference is testable.

### 3.5 · The Discriminatory Test

The ratio contains Δ. The maturation curve does not.

C(a) is a fact about the *child* — about what a person of a given age can do. Nothing in it refers to the parent at all. The ratio, by contrast, is built from both ages; the parent's age enters it through Δ, and the distortion a lagging model produces is, as the geometry showed, proportional to Δ. So hold the child's age fixed — which nails the maturation account in place, since the child's developmental stage is now identical across cases — and vary the one thing the rival says cannot matter: the age of the parent.

Concretely: a twenty-five-year-old with a forty-five-year-old parent (Δ = 20), and the same twenty-five-year-old with a seventy-five-year-old parent (Δ = 50). Repeat the pair at other child-ages. And one constraint of design, stated now rather than discovered later: if the effect of Δ at fixed child-age is real, nothing promises that it is large. A modest effect demands a sample large enough to see it — and "too subtle to measure" will not be accepted here as a way for the prediction to survive its own failure. Three accounts now sign three different predictions, in advance. The maturation account predicts *no effect of parent age*: the child is the same child, at the same stage, and that is all the mechanism can see. The episodic account predicts that whatever moves, moves *both channels together* — a working model is revised or not revised by incidents, and incidents carry capability and standing at once; it has no machinery by which the parent's age could move position while leaving capability untouched. The arithmetic predicts something specific and strange: at a fixed child-age, the larger the gap, the more *junior the positioning* — more deciding-for, more speaking-for, more of the diminutive register — while the capability judgment stays accurate. Distortion of position, scaling with Δ, with knowledge intact.

Two disciplines govern the measurement, and both must be fixed before any data exist, because both are places where a motivated tester could cheat retroactively. First: the error must be measured on the *position* channel — who decides, who is spoken for, what register of address is used — and never as an estimate of capability. This is not a preference but a matter of construction: perceived capability is the very quantity the maturation curve governs, so defining the error there hands the rival the win before the first observation. Second: the position markers themselves must be written down in advance, as a fixed instrument a stranger could apply blind — otherwise the indicators can be chosen after the results, and the test becomes theater.

The test has not been run. That sentence belongs in the book, not in a drawer. The prediction that position-distortion scales with the parent's age at fixed child-age is risky, signed, and quite possibly false — and it is the only cheque the arithmetic has written that the two rivals of this chapter cannot also cash. A third rival, in which the parent's own age enters not as a ratio but as a stake, waits in the next chapter; the discriminator as designed does not yet separate it from the arithmetic.

### 3.6 · What Would Falsify This Idea?

The preface promised that this book would state, out loud, what would show its idea to be wrong. Here is the ledger, and it has three lines, because the idea lives at three levels that must not be allowed to borrow each other's authority.

The first level cannot be falsified, and saying so is not a boast but a boundary. That the ratio collapses while the difference stands still, that its slope shrinks with age, that a lagging model produces ratio-error proportional to Δ — this is derivation, and it falls only if the calculus is wrong. Its certainty is real and it is *cheap*, and the characteristic sin of a book like this one would be to let that cheap certainty leak sideways into the psychology, so that the reader comes away feeling that something about parents has been proved because something about fractions has been. Nothing about parents has been proved in this chapter.

The second level is the descriptive claim — that human beings actually assign relational position by something that tracks the ratio — and it is falsifiable in the ordinary, wholesome way. If, at fixed child-age, the parent's age has no effect on positioning, the arithmetic is not a mechanism. It would then retreat to what the rivals' success would leave it: a mnemonic — a compact, vivid way of restating what maturation and working models already explain — and this book's title would be an overstatement to be corrected, not defended. I write the retreat down now, before any data exist, so that it cannot be renegotiated later by the person with the most to lose from it, who is me.

The third level is the measurement. If the position instrument fails — if the markers of standing refuse to cohere into anything one can score — that failure falsifies neither the mathematics nor the phenomenon; it falsifies a choice about how to look. The honest response would be to rebuild the instrument, and the dishonest one would be to treat the wreckage as noise and promote the claim anyway. The difference between those responses is the difference between a model and a mood.

And one question remains that the ledger cannot answer, because it belongs to the reader. At each prediction in this chapter, ask: *what observation would convince you this model is wrong?* If you cannot name one, you have not been given a model — whatever else you have been given. I have tried to name mine. Until the test is run, the arithmetic remains exactly what the preface said it was: a candidate, on the record, with its kill-line published.


## Chapter 4 · The Markers of Standing

A man of fifty is trying to explain to a friend what happens when he visits his parents. "He still treats me like a boy," he says of his father, and the friend, reasonably, asks what the father does, exactly. And the man discovers that he cannot point to anything. There was no insult. There was no order. Nothing happened that a camera would have flagged. "It's not one thing," he says finally. "It's the whole arrangement." He is almost certainly right, and as stated, his complaint can never be checked by anyone — which means that as stated, it is not evidence of anything. It is the preface's warning come home: a claim that cannot say what would count against it is not a claim. It is a mood.

The last chapter made a demand it did not fulfil. It said that the markers of standing must be written down in advance, as a fixed instrument a stranger could apply blind — and then it went on to other business. This chapter pays the debt. It takes the man's "whole arrangement" and does the one thing his complaint cannot do for itself: it breaks the arrangement into parts that can be pointed to.

One honesty first, because the ground here is not empty. The parent's side of this relationship has been measured before. There are instruments in the literature on which parents report their own legitimate authority over grown children — and report more of it than the children accord them — and there is a research line documenting that parents and their adult children disagree about the criteria for adulthood itself.[^v10] Those instruments measure something real, and something adjacent: they measure *beliefs*, endorsed on questionnaires. What this chapter builds measures something else — *enacted placement*: what is done at the table, on the phone, at the threshold, not what is endorsed on a form. A person's beliefs about authority and a person's conduct at dinner are allowed to differ, and the difference is exactly the kind of thing an instrument should be able to see rather than assume away. A parent can endorse, on a survey, complete faith in an adult child's independence, and still intercept every question addressed to that child across a family dinner; the questionnaire and the table would disagree, and only the table is this chapter's business.

And one design decision, stated before the catalogue because it governs everything in it. Every marker below is a fact of conduct, and only of conduct. "She orders for her daughter" can be scored by a stranger. "She orders because she still thinks of her daughter as a child" cannot — that sentence is not a marker, it is this book's thesis, and the thesis is not allowed inside the ruler. An instrument that requires the observer to infer what someone meant is an instrument that measures the observer. The domains that follow are the ones that survived this rule; they earned their place by yielding at least one observable each, and the catalogue stays open to any domain that can pass the same gate.

### 4.1 · The Catalogue

Begin where observation is cheapest: a phone call.

*The register of address.* "Did you eat? Dress warmly tomorrow. Text me when you land." Notice the grammar before the affection: these are imperatives, not requests, and they arrive without the softening that instructions between equals carry. Notice the diminutives, if the language has them. And then notice the only thing the instrument actually scores — the direction. Does instruction flow one way? When the son says "take your pills, Dad," is it received as care between equals, or met as a small impertinence — answered with "I've been taking pills since before you were born"? A single call contains the whole observable: the flow of imperatives, and what happens when the flow is reversed.

*The voice.* Sunday lunch, a guest at the table, a question addressed to the daughter about her work — and before she has drawn breath, her mother has answered it. Or the family story told two ways, the daughter's version and the mother's version, and the table's quiet settlement on one of them. Who answers questions addressed to whom; whose account of shared events stands when accounts conflict. Both are countable across one meal, by someone who has never met this family. One tie-breaker keeps the marker from drifting into collaborative completion: score interception only when the child is cut off before finishing a response, or when the parent habitually supplies the child's account unprompted — not when a fact is added after the child has spoken.

*The rights of decision.* The type specimen is already on the table — the fish, ordered, in the scene that opened this book. Around it stands a family of verbs: the summer plans that are *announced* rather than *proposed*; the purchase that is *reported* rather than *discussed*; the veto that one party may cast and the other may only wish for. The observable is the grammar of the household calendar: who proposes, who ratifies, who overrules, and in which direction the overruling runs.

*The flow of information.* The parents sell the old apartment, and the daughter learns of it after the deed is signed. Or the mirror case: the son changes jobs, and his parents learn it from an aunt. News travels through a family in a fixed order, and the order is a map of standing that the family draws itself, without knowing it is drawing. The observable is the asymmetry between *consulted before* and *notified after* — who is asked, who is told, and which way the asking runs.

*The rituals of the threshold.* The first year the holiday is held at the son's house instead of the parents'. Who is host and who is guest, in fact and not merely in address; who says the toast; who carves; who sits where without negotiation; whether the parents *ask* what to bring or *announce* what will be cooked in a kitchen that is not theirs. Households are small monarchies, and their tables keep the succession records. The observable is the hosting direction across years, and the ownership of the table's small offices.

*The architecture of the invitation.* The son announces he will not make it for Christmas, and the parents rearrange the date. The parents announce they will not come for Easter, and nothing about the calendar moves. The observable is not the invitation but the response to a refusal: whose absence triggers renegotiation, and whose absence is simply accepted. A refusal that reshapes the calendar is a veto held in reserve. Score as the direction of renegotiation following a declined invitation, across two or three observed occasions — this marker is the mirror of the one that follows: where money tracks the direction of giving, invitation tracks the direction of refusal, and the two are not the same.

*The direction of money.* A folded banknote pressed into the coat pocket of a grown daughter who signs mortgages of her own — and, the same evening, her attempt to pay for dinner refused with something close to indignation. Generosity itself is not the marker; families are generous in every direction. The marker is generosity's *permitted* direction: who may give without ceremony, whose gifts require negotiation, whose repayment is accepted and whose is waved away as if it were a category error — scored, to survive the ordinary theater of a shared bill, only when the refusal is unidirectional and holds across repeated occasions: the parent's gift is never renegotiated, the child's is blocked or reframed as improper every time it is offered.

*The protocol of crisis.* The grandmother falls on a Tuesday. Someone is called first; someone arrives and takes charge; someone is shielded — "don't tell your brother yet, he'll only worry." Call order, command, and the direction of shielding: three observables in one bad afternoon. This marker is the mirror of the first one — the register of address can be scored from any phone call, while a crisis may not oblige for years — which is why the walk began where it began. The instrument's cheap items come first, the way a stranger would actually have to apply it.

Stand back from the walk and one shape is visible in every room of it. Each marker is a *directional asymmetry* in conduct. Instruction has a direction. Interception has a direction. Ratification, consultation, hosting, giving, commanding, shielding — each flows, and can flow either way, or neither. Standing, as this instrument understands it, is nothing more mysterious than what those directions agree on, when they agree.

### 4.2 · The Scoring Discipline

For each marker, the stranger needs three things: the event class to watch for, the direction to record, and the window inside which watching counts. One shared meal is a sufficient window for register, voice, and decision. A season suffices for information and thresholds. Money is scored when it moves. Crisis is scored if the year supplies one, and left blank if it does not — a blank is a fact about opportunity, not about the family. At minimum each marker scores three ways: asymmetry toward one party, asymmetry toward the other, or none observed. Where counting is natural — interceptions in an afternoon — the score may be a count. Nothing in any of this requires knowing who these people are to each other beyond the two names.

Now the exclusions, because an instrument is defined as much by what it refuses to measure. Warmth is not a marker. The frequency of visits is not a marker. Expressed pride is not a marker, and neither is generosity as such — only its permitted direction is. These exclusions are not a judgment that closeness does not matter; they are a firewall. There is a rival explanation of family life — it will get its full day in court in the next chapter — that predicts warmth wherever it predicts anything. An instrument that scored warmth as juniority would hand that rival a free confound, and every measurement made with it would be arguing in a circle.

And the rule beneath the exclusions, stated once in the open: the instrument reads conduct, not hearts. It scores who answered the question addressed to the daughter. It does not score why.

### 4.3 · Two Confusions, Designed Out

Two pairs of things travel together often enough to be mistaken for each other, and the instrument is built to pull each pair apart. The proof that two axes are genuinely two is a person who scores high on one and low on the other, so here are four such people.

The father who hands his son the family taxes every March — his son the accountant, and there is no fuller endorsement of a man's competence than your own unopened envelopes — and who, in the same week, announces to him the dates of the family holiday. The competence file is current. The standing is junior. And the mirror: the parents who consult their son on every family decision, full peers in the grammar of the calendar, and who nonetheless re-explain to him, every visit, the road to the coast he has driven for twenty years. The standing is current; the file of what he can do lags behind. One axis can move while the other stands still, in either order — which is precisely the claim the restaurant scene made in Chapter 1, now visible at instrument level.

Then the second pair. A distant father — two calls a year, no warmth anyone would testify to — who has never once decided for his son and speaks to him as to a colleague. Closeness low; standing full. And an adoring mother who celebrates her daughter to everyone, keeps her photograph in every room — and answers the waiter. Closeness high; standing junior. If the instrument cannot score these four people correctly, it is broken. Scoring them correctly is what the exclusion list is for.

### 4.4 · What the Instrument Cannot See

Three limits, plainly, because an instrument that does not state its blind spots is claiming eyes it does not have.

First, the cultural baseline. In some languages the diminutive is the default register of tenderness for the entire family; in some houses, imperatives are simply how everyone loves everyone. Absolute counts mean nothing across families. The instrument reads *asymmetry within* a family and *change over time* within the same pair — never levels compared between one family and another. A stranger scoring a family scores it against itself.

Second, conduct is not belief. A parent may enact perfect equality at every table and hold, privately, the whole junior arrangement intact in their head — and the instrument is blind to it. This blindness is the direct price of the conduct rule, and it is paid knowingly: an instrument that tried to see hearts would see mostly its own assumptions. What is lost in depth is bought back in checkability, and this book has already declared which of the two it values when they conflict.

Third, the window is armor against noise. One bad Sunday is not a standing; one intercepted answer is an event, not a structure. The windows exist so that a mood is never scored as an arrangement.

One domain was considered and excluded, and the exclusion is recorded rather than left for a reader to wonder about: *the microphysics of correction* — who corrects whose facts or wording, "no, it was Tuesday, not Wednesday." It looks directional and observable, and it fails the conduct rule on inspection. Scoring a correction as a marker of standing requires knowing whether the correction is *true* — without that, the instrument records who talks more, or whose memory is better, not who stands where. The domain was not forgotten. It was tried, and it did not survive its own rule.

### 4.5 · The Hand-off

What a book can do for an instrument ends here. What remains is work outside these pages: an item pool written from these domains; raters who know neither the family nor this book; agreement between raters checked and reported; and the outcome accepted in advance if the markers refuse to cohere — that failure mode already has its place in the previous chapter's ledger, and it falsifies the measuring, not the phenomenon.

One sentence carries the whole contract, and it is the reason this chapter exists as prose rather than as an appendix: the instrument is fixed before any data exist, and this chapter is the timestamp.

And one last sentence, owed to the invariant this chapter was built under. Whatever the test eventually says about the number from Chapter 1, these pages do not change. A way of seeing who stands where in a family is useful in any world — including the one in which this book's central idea turns out to be wrong.

## Chapter 5 · The Stake

Ask a mother how close she feels to her grown son. Then ask the son the same question about her. The two answers very often fail to match, and they do not fail at random: hers is the higher one. She reports more closeness, more affection, more agreement about the things that matter. He reports less. Neither of them is lying, and neither is confused. They are describing the same relationship from inside it, and they are describing it differently.

This is not an anecdote and not a fragile result. It is one of the most durable findings in the study of families across the lifespan, it has been replicated across decades and across generations, and it has a name: the *intergenerational stake*.[^v11] The parent has more invested in the relationship than the child does, and the difference shows up in how each of them reports it.

I have to take this seriously for a reason that has nothing to do with fairness to the literature. The last chapter promised this rival its day in court, and the chapter before it left a hole that this rival walks straight into. The arithmetic's one distinguishing prediction — that at a fixed child-age, a larger gap produces more junior positioning — was offered as the only cheque the rivals could not also cash. The stake can cash it. That is the business of this chapter, and I would rather state it in the first hundred words than let it arrive as a reviewer's discovery.

### 5.1 · The Rival at Full Strength

Bengtson and Kuypers proposed, in 1971, that the two generations in a family have unequal investments in each other, and that the inequality is developmental rather than personal. The parent, further along, sees in the child a continuation — of a name, of values, of the work of a life, of the self that will not otherwise persist. The child, at the opposite end of the same passage, is engaged in the ordinary and necessary business of becoming a separate person, and a separate person is precisely one who does not describe himself as a continuation of anyone. The asymmetry is not a flaw in either party. It is what it looks like when two people stand at different points of the same road and describe the distance between them.

The empirical residue of that idea has outlasted the explanation offered for it, and I want to be exact about which part is load-bearing. What is well documented is the *phenomenon*: parents perceive the shared relationship more favourably than their children do, consistently, across studies and across time. What has fared less well is the original theoretical justification — the developmental account of *why* — which has not accumulated the support the phenomenon itself has. This matters for how the rival must be built. It cannot be built on its mechanism, because its mechanism is unsettled. It must be built on its finding, which is robust, and a robust finding is quite enough to sink a competing explanation.

So build it. Return to the restaurant, and let the stake account tell the scene.

The mother orders for her daughter. On the stake account, nothing has gone wrong with her picture of her daughter at all — no lag, no freeze, no error of any kind. What is happening is that she is exercising a role, and the role is the most valuable thing she owns. To be a mother is to be the one who knows what her daughter can and cannot eat. That knowledge is not a claim about food; it is a certificate of standing, and the standing is worth more to her than it is to her daughter, because she has more invested in it. She orders the fish because ordering the fish is what a mother does, and because a woman who no longer does what a mother does is no longer, in the only sense that matters to her, a mother. The daughter experiences it as being made nine years old. The mother is not making anyone nine. She is holding onto the one office in which she is irreplaceable.

Notice how much of this book that explanation covers. It covers the warmth — the scene is warm because the investment is real, and no account that treats the mother as merely careless can say why she is loving as she does it. It covers the imperviousness to fact: correcting the mother's estimate of her daughter's competence would change nothing, because her competence was never the point; the role was. It covers the asymmetry of the two channels, and covers it elegantly — the capability file updates freely because nothing is at stake in it, while the position channel is defended because position *is* the stake. And it covers, without any arithmetic whatever, the thing Chapter 1 found strangest: that a parent can know everything about a grown child and still place her as junior. Of course she can. Knowing is free. Placing is what she is unwilling to give up.

That is the rival, and it is the strongest one this book has met. The maturation account explained the age gradient but had nothing to say about why the freeze persists into a child's fifties. The episodic account explained the persistence but could not separate the channels. The stake explains the gradient, the persistence, the separation of the channels, and the warmth — with one motive, no geometry, and sixty years of replication behind its central observation.

### 5.2 · Why It Signs My Prediction

It would be convenient if the stake stopped there, because an account that explains everything except the one thing I have staked my claim on is not a competitor for the claim. But it does not stop there, and the reason is worth setting out slowly, because it is the reason this chapter exists.

The stake is not a constant. It is an investment, and investments have terms. The parent of forty-five has decades in which the relationship may still be renegotiated, repaired, revisited; the parent of seventy-five does not. Time remaining is itself a component of what a stake is worth, and time remaining shrinks. Add to that the plain accumulation — more years given, more given up, more of a life whose meaning now rests on how this one relationship turned out — and a conclusion follows that I want to mark rather than slide past, because everything else in this chapter rests on it: *the older parent has more at stake in the same relationship than the younger parent does.*

That step is my inference from the construct, not a finding I can cite. I have not verified that Bengtson and Kuypers assert it, nor that the subsequent literature establishes it.[^v11] The phenomenon they found is robust; its age-linkage at fixed child-age is my extension of it. And the direction of my interest here is worth naming, since a reader cannot see it and I can: if the age-linkage fails, the rival built in this chapter weakens and Chapter 3's discriminator recovers. That would be convenient for me, which is precisely why the step belongs in the body of the chapter it carries, and not only in a note at the back where a reader who trusts the argument would never look for it.

Now put that into the design of Chapter 3. Hold the child's age fixed at twenty-five. Compare a parent of forty-five with a parent of seventy-five. The maturation account is nailed in place, exactly as intended — the child is the same child, at the same stage. But the stake is not held in place at all. The seventy-five-year-old has more invested and less time; the stake account predicts he will defend the role more; defending the role means deciding for, speaking for, instructing. More junior positioning, at fixed child-age, as a function of the parent's age.

That is my prediction. Word for word, at the same ages, in the same direction.

I want to be precise about what has just happened, because it is not the ordinary case of a rival that must be argued down. Chapter 3 stated three accounts and showed they signed three different predictions, and that demonstration was correct as far as it went. The stake signs a fourth prediction that is indistinguishable from the third. The discriminatory test, as designed, does not discriminate. It was built to rule out an account in which the parent's age cannot matter, and it succeeds at that; it is silent against an account in which the parent's age matters for a reason that is not arithmetic at all.

This objection did not arrive before the test was written. It arrived after, and it found a hole. I record that in the order it happened, because a book that presents every objection as one it had already anticipated is not being careful; it is being flattered by its own chronology.

### 5.3 · Where the Two Come Apart

A rival that signs the same prediction is not yet a rival that cannot be separated. It means only that the separation is not where I first put it. There are two places where these accounts differ, and both of them are usable.

The first is that the stake is *directly measurable, and Δ is not a proxy for it.* This is the more important of the two, so let me put it plainly. The arithmetic works through a quantity that is a property of the pair and nothing else: the difference of two birth years, fixed, public, and utterly uninformative about anyone's inner life. The stake works through a quantity that is a property of a person — how much this relationship matters to him, how much of himself he takes it to contain, how much time he believes he has left — and every one of those has been measured before, by instruments that exist. The two accounts therefore make different predictions about what happens when the stake is measured and held constant. If the parent-age effect on positioning is the stake wearing a costume, then measuring the stake directly and controlling for it should make the effect of Δ disappear. If the effect of Δ survives the control, there is something in the gap that investment does not account for.

I am aware of what I have just written, so let me write the other half of it before anyone else does. That test can go against me, and if it does, it goes against me cleanly. A world in which controlling for measured stake absorbs the entire Δ effect is a world in which this book's central idea is decoration on a motivational account that was published when I was not yet born. I would rather that be settled by measurement than by whoever argues longer, and I am signing it here, before the data exist, for the same reason Chapter 3 signed its retreat: a prediction that can only be evaluated after the result is known is not a prediction.

The second difference is in the *shape of what each account predicts*, and it is where the previous chapter turns out to have been building a defence before the attack was named.

The stake's signature finding is favourability. The parent perceives the relationship as warmer, closer, more mutual than the child does; that is what the phenomenon *is*, in the literature where it was found. So an account of the restaurant built on the stake predicts a parent who is not only positioning her daughter as junior but is also — measurably, on report — feeling and describing the relationship more warmly than her daughter does. The arithmetic predicts nothing about warmth. It predicts placement, and it is indifferent to affection: a cold parent with a large gap should position as junior, and a devoted parent with a small gap should not, and the arithmetic is content with both.

Which means the discriminating cases are the mismatched ones, and Chapter 4 has already made them scorable. The distant father who has never decided for his son — low stake, and standing intact. The adoring mother who answers the waiter — high warmth, and standing junior. These two people were put in the previous chapter as a test of whether an instrument could tell closeness from position; they now do a second job, which is to tell the stake from the arithmetic. And the exclusion rule that chapter insisted on — that warmth is never scored as juniority, that the frequency of visits and expressed pride are firewalled out of the instrument — was not fastidiousness. Had the instrument scored warmth as juniority, the stake and the arithmetic could never have been distinguished by anything it measured, because every observation would have been consistent with both. The firewall was built before the rival was named, and it is the reason the rival can be tested at all.

There is a third difference, and I list it last because I trust it least. The arithmetic predicts a specific functional form: distortion proportional to Δ, because that is what the geometry delivers and it has no freedom to deliver anything else. The stake predicts monotonicity — more investment, more defence of the role — but derives no shape. In principle, strict linearity in Δ favours the arithmetic and a curve that tracks measured investment rather than the raw gap favours the stake. In practice, distinguishing functional forms requires far more data than distinguishing the presence of an effect, and I would not want the argument to rest here. It is a tiebreaker, not a test.

### 5.4 · The Uncomfortable Possibility

There is a reading of all this in which the question of the chapter is malformed, and honesty costs nothing here, so let me put it up.

Suppose the two accounts are not rivals. Suppose the arithmetic is the *reason* the stake behaves as it does — that what makes an older parent's investment feel larger is precisely that the child occupies a smaller and smaller proportion of a life that is itself running out, so that the ratio is not competing with the stake but generating it. Or suppose the reverse: that the felt sense of investment is the primitive, and the ratio is a way of describing it after the fact, a piece of arithmetic that happens to run parallel to something psychological and has no work of its own to do.

On the first reading, "which of the two is true" is the wrong question, and the right one is which sits upstream — and if the ratio is what sits upstream, this book has not dressed up a 1971 finding; it has found what was quietly generating it. On the second reading, my book is a well-drawn map of someone else's territory. I cannot separate these possibilities with the design as it stands, and I do not know a design that would separate them cleanly. What I can do is refuse to let the ambiguity work quietly in my favour, which is what it would do if I left it out — a reader who is not told that two accounts might be one is a reader who will assume they are two, and count my rival's every success as my own.

### 5.5 · What This Chapter Settles, and What It Does Not

It does not defeat the stake. It was not going to; a finding replicated for half a century is not defeated by a chapter, and an author who claims to have done it in ten pages should be read with suspicion for the rest of the book.

What it does is smaller and, I think, worth more. It names the strongest rival this idea has, at full strength and in its own terms. It concedes without hedging that the test of Chapter 3, as designed, could not tell that rival from the arithmetic. And it repairs the design rather than the claim: the measurement now requires the parent's stake to be measured directly and held constant, and it requires the outcome to be read on the position channel with warmth firewalled out — the second requirement having been built, by luck or by the logic of the thing, one chapter before it was needed.

The price is that the test is now more expensive and considerably more dangerous to me. Chapter 3 offered a prediction that two rivals could not make. This chapter has admitted a third rival that makes it, and has bought the discrimination back only at the cost of a control that could take the whole effect away. That is the correct direction for a test to move: away from the version I would win, toward the version that could settle something.

And the retreat, since it belongs in the book and not in a drawer. If measured stake absorbs the parent-age effect entirely, the arithmetic does not survive as a mechanism. It would then be what the second level of Chapter 3's ledger already described — a compact and vivid way of restating something the field found first, in 1971, in a journal of gerontology, while I was busy being someone's child.

But notice what that outcome would not undo. It would not undo the two channels — capability and position, moving at different speeds, visible apart in the same parent at the same table — because that separation was made in Chapter 1 and scored in Chapter 4, on grounds the stake never contested; the stake explains *why* position might freeze, not *whether* it is a different quantity from capability, and it was never asked to answer that. It would not undo the instrument, which reads conduct and not motive, and does not care which name the underlying mechanism ends up wearing. And it would not undo the design of this chapter's test — a discriminator built so that the stake's own signature, favourability, must come apart from position before either account can claim the result. Losing the mechanism would not mean losing the chapter's work. It would mean the ratio was a lens rather than an engine. A lens precise enough to put a sixty-year-old finding to a real test is not nothing, even on the day it stops being called an engine.

## Epilogue

If your son or daughter appeared before you today as a stranger — without your knowing they were your child — would you speak to them the way you speak to them now?

If the answer is no, perhaps it is not the child who has stayed behind. Perhaps it is your image of the child.

Whether the same thing happens wherever one person's model of another was built early and never rebuilt is a question this book has deliberately not asked; that is a different book.

## Register of Claims

*State as of 2026-07-26.*

The notes to this book record the status of its sources: which have been verified against primary texts, which are still carried on a secondary summary, which were checked and found wrong. This register does the same for its claims.

It exists because the chapters were written in sequence and each one attacks the one before it. A claim can therefore be superseded thirty pages after it is made, and a reader moving forward through the book will hold, for those thirty pages, a position the book has already left. Where an entry below reads *superseded*, the earlier text has deliberately not been rewritten. Revising Chapter 3 to reflect what Chapter 5 discovered would produce a book that had anticipated every objection in advance, which is exactly the flattery Chapter 5 refuses on its own behalf. The chapters stand in the order they were thought. This register carries the corrections.

**L1 · The geometry.** That the difference of two ages (Δ, fixed at birth) is constant while their ratio collapses; that the ratio's slope has magnitude Δ/a², where a is the child's age; that a lagging model produces ratio-error proportional to Δ. **Derivation** (§3.1). Neither falsifiable nor confirmable by observation; it falls only if the calculus is wrong. Unchanged since first statement. Its certainty is real and cheap, and §3.6 names the characteristic sin of letting cheap certainty leak sideways into the psychology.

**L2 · The phenomenon.** That a parent's picture of a grown child can stay fixed while the facts move on. **Not mine — closed in the field's favour** (Ch. 2). Studied since 1965 on the child's side and since the mid-2000s on the parent's; named, and measured by a validated instrument. Chapter 2 is a concession, not a claim.

**L3 · Two channels.** That capability and position are distinct representations able to update independently. **Open, supported, uncontested** (§3.2; made scorable §4.3; reaffirmed §5.5). An existence claim resting on the scene in Chapter 1 and on the four mismatched people in §4.3. No rival in this book contests it: the stake explains why position might freeze, not whether it is a different quantity from capability, and it was never asked to. This is the claim that survives every adverse outcome listed below.

**L4 · Whether the split is already modelled.** Whether the literature anywhere models knowledge-versus-placement as two representations moving at two speeds. **Open** (Ch. 2; note v4b). The nearest instrument was verified and resolves into two factors — comprehending, and letting go — both of which concern the parent's *adjustment* rather than this distinction; but their existence does not prove the distinction unmodelled elsewhere. Closing this requires a lineage audit of parental-representation models, which has not been done. Carried forward as a debt, to be paid before the book's strongest claim rather than after it.

**L5 · The descriptive claim.** That human beings assign relational position by something tracking the ratio. **Falsifiable, untested** (§3.6, second level). Retreat published in advance: if the parent's age has no effect on positioning at fixed child-age, the arithmetic is not a mechanism, and this book's title is an overstatement to be corrected rather than defended.

**L6 · The discriminator.** That at fixed child-age, a larger gap produces more junior positioning while the capability judgment stays accurate. **Superseded as a discriminator** (§3.5, superseded by §5.2). As designed in Chapter 3 it separates the arithmetic from the maturation account and from the episodic account, and §3.5 claims it as the only cheque those two cannot also cash. That claim does not survive Chapter 5: the intergenerational stake signs the same prediction, at the same ages, in the same direction. **Absence of the effect still falsifies L5; presence of it no longer confirms.** The design was repaired rather than the claim withdrawn — §5.3 now requires the parent's stake to be measured directly and held constant, with the outcome read on the position channel and warmth firewalled out.

**L7 · The age-linkage of stake.** That an older parent holds a larger stake than a younger one, in the same relationship, at the same child-age. **Open — the author's inference, unverified** (§5.2; note v11). Not a finding that can be cited. The whole of Chapter 5's rivalry rests on it. If it fails, the rival weakens and L6 partially recovers — an outcome convenient to me, which is why the step is marked in the body of Chapter 5 and not only here.

**L8 · The instrument.** That the markers of standing cohere and can be scored blind by a stranger who knows neither the family nor this book. **Untested** (§3.6, third level; hand-off §4.5). An item pool, independent raters, and reported inter-rater agreement are work outside these pages. Failure here falsifies the looking rather than the thing looked at, and the response fixed in advance is to rebuild the instrument, not to treat the wreckage as noise.

**L9 · Upstream and downstream.** Whether the ratio is what generates the stake, or the felt investment is primitive and the ratio merely a description of it after the fact. **Acknowledged unresolvable under the present design** (§5.4). No design is offered that would separate the two cleanly. Recorded so that the ambiguity cannot work quietly in the author's favour, which is what it would do if left out.

**L10 · The wider case.** Whether the same failure occurs wherever one person's model of another was built early and never rebuilt. **Deliberately not asked** (Epilogue). Outside this book's scope by choice; nothing here argues for it or against it.

The test has not been run. Nothing in this register reports a result, because there are none yet to report.
